Let me start with a problem that happens every day in research, and that almost nobody catches.

When a researcher writes a paper, they cite other papers. Often forty of them. Every citation is a promise. It says, this other paper backs up what I just claimed.

Almost nobody checks those promises. Not because researchers are careless, but because checking one properly takes about twenty minutes. You have to find the source, read it, and compare what it really says against what the new paper says it says. Forty citations is more than a day of work, so it does not get done.

So things slip through. A number gets copied across, but the conditions around it quietly fall away. A paper gets retracted years later, and nobody tells the people who already cited it.

PaperTrail checks all of it, and it shows you its work.

It does two jobs.

The first job is checking a paper you already have. You give it a PDF, and it tells you which claims hold up, which do not, and which it could not check at all.

The second job starts from a question you have not answered yet. It reads the field for you, finds what nobody has settled, and helps you write a first draft, using only sources it has already checked.

Both jobs use the same team. Let me show you that team.

There are thirty-two agents. Each one lives in its own file, and each one does a single job.

Four of them work at the same time. One group follows citations. One group reads the numbers. One group looks at the method. And one group gathers similar papers to compare against.

The part I am most pleased with is how a citation gets judged. Instead of asking one agent whether a citation is correct, three agents handle it. The first one argues that the citation fails. The second argues that it holds. Neither can see what the other wrote. Then a third agent reads both arguments and decides. One agent asked to judge its own reading will usually agree with itself. Three agents arguing will not.

The numbers work the same way. Two agents read every number separately, without seeing each other's work. That is exactly how a careful review does it with two people. If the two disagree, a judge settles it from the source, and the disagreement is reported rather than hidden.
